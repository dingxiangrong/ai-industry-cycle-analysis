## I. Core Underlying Definition: Downstream Large-Model Enterprises Are Not Asset-Light, but Bear Rigid Liabilities via Installment Payments for Computing Resources
The industry commonly labels OpenAI and Anthropic as asset-light AI software companies, which represents a typical cognitive fallacy. The defining feature of genuine asset-light business models is cost elasticity: expenditures can be flexibly reduced in response to market conditions and business scale to mitigate operational risks.

For downstream large-model enterprises, however, computing power is the sole core production factor and an indispensable rigid input. Without computing resources, model inference, API calls and end-user services cannot be delivered, bringing business operations to a halt. Rather than building data centers, developing proprietary chips or constructing computing-power infrastructure, these firms sign multi-year long-term lock-in contracts with third-party computing providers. Their business essence is not flexible leasing, but large-scale installment payments for the right to use computing resources.

This model carries three irreversible cost constraints:
1. Forward contracts lock in cash outflows for years ahead with fixed non-reducible amounts, offering zero operational flexibility.
2. Leased computing resources incorporate equipment depreciation, capital interest, vendor profit margins and operation-and-maintenance expenses. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) far exceeds that of in-house computing infrastructure built by large tech groups.
3. Early contract termination incurs heavy penalty fees, eliminating opportunities for cost-cutting loss containment amid business downturns.

Industrial realities as of August 2026 fully validate this logic. Anthropic signed three new long-term computing contracts within a single month: a USD-45-billion 6-year deal with Nscale in West Virginia, a USD-35-billion 6-year agreement with Lambda in Texas, and a USD-10-billion order with Volta in Norway. Newly locked-in exposure reached approximately USD-90-billion for that month. Together with existing agreements, Anthropic¡¯s cumulative forward-locked computing-power liabilities for 2026 exceeded USD-135-billion. Its annualized run-rate revenue stood at only USD-65-billion, yielding a rigid computing-liability coverage ratio above 2¡Á. Many of these obligations had not yet been formally classified as rigid off-balance-sheet liabilities prior to S-1 filing, creating substantial financial-statement uncertainty.

A fatal structural contradiction emerges: corporate revenue and market demand are volatile variables subject to growth, stagnation or contraction. Installment-based computing-power expenses, by contrast, represent fixed rigid liabilities with permanent long-term cost exposure. This creates core risks whereby revenue growth may fail to outpace cost expansion.

## II. Three-Tier Demand Structure of the AI Industry: Four Structural Flaws Undermine the Survival Foundation of Pure-Model Enterprises
All commercially viable AI business cycles rely on three core categories of demand: consumer-facing To-C personal demand, enterprise-focused To-B commercial demand, and internal self-use demand from large technology groups. Continuous evolution and restructuring of these demand streams, compounded by four irreversible structural flaws, dismantle the growth logic of pure-model firms and compress supposed long-term risks into an 18-30-month short-term horizon.

### 1. Dual-Layer Bubbles Undermine Valuation Logic: Not Merely Narrative Hype, but Expectations of Substantial Insolvency Driven by Gross-Margin Illusions and Liability-Maturity Mismatch
Market discourse frames risks facing independent AI-model firms solely as valuation bubbles. In reality, dual rigid bubbles exist at both operational and financial levels, lacking fundamental support typical of growth-oriented technology enterprises. Profitability data show OpenAI¡¯s gross margin fell from 40-% to 33-% in 2025; inference costs quadrupled year-on-year to USD-8.4-billion (cross-validated using Sacra estimates and leaked FT financial statements). Every USD-1 of generated revenue consumed USD-0.70 in rigid computing-power expenditure. Even with gross margins near 40-%, Anthropic sustained multi-billion-dollar annual net cash burn. Book profits constituted only temporary illusions stemming from deferred settlement of computing-power contracts.

From a valuation perspective, following Anthropic¡¯s Series-H financing in May-2026, annualized revenue run-rate reached approximately USD-47-billion against a valuation of USD-965-billion. By July the run-rate climbed to USD-65-billion, yet gross margins remained stuck around 40-%, with stable annual net cash burn of USD-30-50-billion. The scale-diseconomy pattern ¡ª surging revenue alongside stagnant gross margins and persistent burn ¡ª became entrenched. Its valuation multiple remained as high as 20¡Á revenue. Mature global SaaS firms deliver steady-state gross margins of 70-80-% with reasonable valuation multiples of 10-15¡Á. Pure-model companies essentially operate as cloud-computing resellers with no core proprietary assets, no pricing power and no cost moats. Their fair valuation multiple should lie within 4-8¡Á revenue. Current valuations draw entirely on AI-industry narratives that discount future performance.

Most damaging is asset-liability maturity mismatch. Corporate revenue consists of volatile monthly and quarterly API-derived income streams, while computing-power commitments represent rigid multi-year liabilities locked over 3-5-year horizons. Tightened fundraising windows or rising market interest rates can trigger valuation halving and cash-flow collapse even without operational deterioration, driven purely by balance-sheet imbalances. Three layers of paper-based illusions sustain current performance: revenue growth achieved via token-price cuts to drive usage volumes, book-keeping profits covered by subsequent financing rounds, and inflated valuations underpinned by industry storytelling.

### 2. Irreversible Diversion of Model Demand: Two Trackways Permanently Constrain Viable Market Space (In-House Closed-Loops at Large Tech Firms + Displacement by Traditional IT Industry Upgrades)
Demand diversion is often misinterpreted merely as attrition of peripheral customers. In truth, two parallel forces ¡ª closed-loop in-house development by technology giants and upgrades within the traditional IT sector ¡ª permanently erode the core addressable market for pure-model enterprises. This represents an irreversible industrial-structural shift and the root cause behind the non-viability of pure intermediary-layer firms.

#### Dimension One: Closed-Loop In-House Development within Large-Tech Ecosystems Eliminates High-End Incremental Demand
Microsoft-360 serves as a prominent illustration. Massive AI-call volumes originating within Excel, Outlook, Teams and the full Copilot suite once formed core To-B incremental business for OpenAI and Anthropic. These workloads have since migrated in-house to Microsoft¡¯s self-developed MAI family of small-to-medium models. Microsoft publicly disclosed that MAI-series models deliver GPU-inference-cost reductions of up to 84-89-% for high-frequency commercial scenarios including Copilot, Office and Dynamics. Extrapolated from historical external large-model call volumes, office and software-development use-cases alone yield billions of US-dollars in annual savings on third-party-model procurement for Microsoft.

Global technology giants have converged on consistent development roadmaps: Google discontinued third-party-model access in favour of its proprietary Antigravity stack; Meta replaced external services across its product portfolio with open-source in-house Llama models; domestic leaders including ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent completed deployment of internally-built models. Meanwhile mature mid-tier and lightweight models such as Phi, Llama, Qwen, GLM and Kimi have become widely available. Enterprises commonly deploy LiteLLM routing layers to orchestrate heterogeneous model workloads. 80-% of standardised lightweight tasks now run on in-house or open-source models, reserving high-end large-model invocations for only the most complex reasoning tasks.

This diversion trend cannot be reversed. In-house models operated by giants incur marginal costs limited to electricity and hardware depreciation. By contrast, third-party-model procurement perpetuates brand-premium payments and vendor margins. Having established closed-loop capabilities, large firms will not revert to external sourcing and paying a persistent ¡°model tax¡±. The core To-B customer base of pure-model enterprises is permanently hollowed-out. In April-2026 Microsoft converted its exclusive partnership with OpenAI to a non-exclusive framework, signalling the definitive shift among large-tech players toward the end-state architecture of ¡°in-house development first, external procurement supplementary¡±. In-house closed-loop strategies block incremental-demand pathways for pure-model vendors.

#### Dimension Two: Full-Scale Upgrades within Traditional IT Replace Existing Delivery-Oriented Markets
Beyond losing incremental business from large-tech buyers, pure-model enterprises are locked out of trillion-dollar legacy informatisation markets covering government-public-sector clients, finance, manufacturing and healthcare. Success in these verticals depends far less upon algorithmic performance than upon domain know-how, compliance frameworks, legacy-system integration and full-lifecycle delivery-and-operation capabilities. These represent inherent genetic weaknesses for pure-model startups yet constitute century-old core moats for traditional IT service providers including Huawei, telecom operators, Softstone, China National Software, Neusoft, Accenture and IBM.

Between 2025 and 2026 legacy IT vendors completed comprehensive embedded-AI upgrades, producing mature ¡°industry-specific delivery plus lightweight-AI¡± solutions that displace generic outsourced-API offerings from pure-model firms. For use-cases such as government digital transformation, industrial MES/ERP modernisation, financial compliance risk control and medical-image diagnostics, public-sector and enterprise customers prioritise compatibility, regulatory compliance, stability and operational guarantees over extreme model-parameter benchmarks. Traditional IT suppliers embed open-source or lightweight models inside vertical-industry solutions and capture down-market To-B opportunities through low-margin, highly-adaptable, deployable offerings.

Unlike pure-online-API-focused pure-model companies lacking delivery capacity, after-sales support and vertical-domain adaptation, traditional IT service providers maintain local-site teams, long-standing government-enterprise trust credentials and legacy-system compatibility expertise. They can execute private deployments, business-process re-engineering, compliance remediation and year-round operational guarantees to satisfy real-world industrial-implementation requirements. As of September-2026, over 95-% of government-enterprise AI procurement orders correspond to customised ¡°IT solution plus embedded-AI¡± projects. Purchases of generic raw-model APIs account for less than 5-% of orders and continue contracting rapidly.

The dual-track squeeze locks in final market dynamics: large-tech in-house closed-loops cut-off high-end incremental demand from above; traditional-IT-sector upgrades monopolise legacy industrial-implementation markets from below. Pure-model firms are caught in between, excluded both from large-tech-generated internal traffic dividends and from vertical-industry implementation contracts. The intermediate generic-API market devolves into a hyper-competitive red ocean with no independent-survival niches remaining.

### 3. Collapse of the Only Viable High-End Stable-Demand Market: Self-Closure of Software-Development-Scenario Demand Sources Shuts Down the Demand Generator
The AI-powered high-end software-development scenario exemplified by Claude-Code was once regarded as the sole resilient high-stickiness moat for pure-model enterprises and contributed materially to Anthropic¡¯s core revenue. Industrial-source-logic analysis indicates this segment will in fact be among the earliest to undergo shake-out with minimal long-term survival prospects.

Primary sources of AI-software-development demand are not small-and-medium outsourcing houses, but global top-tier technology giants including Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon, Alibaba and ByteDance. Historically these firms outsourced substantial volumes of coding, system-iteration and migration work to external software vendors, who in turn purchased programming-capable models such as GPT and Claude, forming a high-end revenue base for pure-model suppliers.

Today all major groups build in-house coding-specialised models: Microsoft¡¯s MAI-Code-1-Flash is deeply integrated within GitHub-Copilot; Google¡¯s Antigravity-CLI ships native programming capabilities; Meta iterates the Code-Llama workflow; Amazon delivers an end-to-end stack combining Trainium chips, proprietary Nova models and the Bedrock platform. In-house tooling boosts internal engineering productivity and directly reduces external software-outsourcing spend. A fatal causal chain emerges: **Large-tech in-house closed-loop development ¡ú fewer outsourced-software orders ¡ú outsourcing firms discontinue high-end programming-API subscriptions ¡ú pure-model enterprises lose core high-end demand**.

The underlying issue is not mere customer churn; the very source of demand is deactivated. Amazon¡¯s partial reliance on third-party models today reflects only a transitional traffic-management phase. Its trajectory from e-commerce leader to top-tier cloud provider with custom silicon provides clear precedent: Amazon will inevitably complete full in-house-model closure and phase-out third-party alternatives. Software-development capability is not an external high-value moat; it represents core intellectual-property defence for large-tech R&D organisations that will not permit sustained taxation by outside vendors.

### 4. From Blue Ocean to Red Ocean: Structural Diseconomies of Scale Where Revenue-Growth Rates Permanently Trail Cost-Growth Rates
2023-2024 represented a short-lived blue-ocean phase for AI-model businesses characterised by limited competitors, high token pricing and willingness among enterprise trial customers to pay premium rates. This created a temporary mirage of high growth and healthy gross margins. By 2025-2026 the industry descended into cut-throat red-ocean competition. Prices for models delivering equivalent intelligence fell 90-97-% year-on-year as price wars erode revenue floors.

Meanwhile inference and training costs remain rigid and trend upward. Spurious token consumption within complex reasoning-oriented models plus task-adaptation overheads push inference-cost levels higher. Industry projections estimate OpenAI¡¯s 2027 inference-cost burden will reach USD-57-billion; Anthropic¡¯s stable annual rigid cash burn runs at USD-30-50-billion. A stark diseconomy-of-scale paradox manifests: as Anthropic¡¯s annualised revenue expanded from USD-1-billion to USD-65-billion, gross margins failed to improve and remained permanently trapped near 40-%. While OpenAI revenue repeatedly doubled, its gross margin contracted by 7 percentage-points.

Cost drivers trend persistently upward: flagship-model training runs cost USD-0.5-1-billion apiece; multi-year locked-in computing-power commitments impose multi-billion-dollar fixed obligations; free-user-traffic subsidies and spurious implicit-reasoning-token overheads add further expense. Revenue streams face continuous pressure from industry price competition, demand segmentation and erosion of high-end scenarios. No pathway toward stable profitability is visible within this business model. Challenges are not cyclical growing-pains but an irreversible structural dead-end.

## III. Recurrence of Industrial End-State Logic: AI Follows Internet-Era Principles ¡ª End-User Experience Is King; Intermediary Layers Are Doomed
Industry-wide reshuffling, competition and shake-out within AI do not break fundamental iterative patterns established across twenty years of internet-industry evolution. Most market commentators fixate on the ¡°AI technological revolution¡± while overlooking key historical commonality: industrial revolutions ultimately deliver products and services directly to end-users and eliminate intermediate layers.

Internet-era winners are platforms achieving direct end-user reach: Google Search, WeChat, Douyin, Meta. Intermediate channels, web portals, SP service providers and traffic brokers were systematically eliminated. One axiom governed the internet industry: **Those controlling end-user touch-points, user-facing scenarios and distribution channels dominate. Entities confined to intermediate roles as suppliers, channels or tool resellers devolve into excess capacity.**

Today¡¯s AI industry represents an accelerated rerun of that internet-era end-state. Only the timeline has been compressed to 18-30-months by computing-power-liability dynamics. Large models are intermediate technology components rather than end-user scenarios. Microsoft, Google, ByteDance, Alibaba and Amazon pursue in-house-model development not out of technical-race enthusiasm, but because end-user sovereignty cannot be outsourced to third parties. Microsoft controls 600-million Office end-users; Google serves over 2-billion search-traffic users; ByteDance operates an 800-million-daily-active short-video ecosystem. These giants¡¯ core assets are direct-to-user scenarios; models serve only as underlying enabling components.

Tying end-user-facing scenarios to externally-sourced models means ceding user workflows, datasets and intelligent-distribution authority to external intermediaries ¡ª a strategic loss unacceptable to large-tech organisations. In-house-model development therefore represents an irreversible strategic imperative: better to burn capital building internally than depend upon external suppliers.

The fatal flaw of pure-model firms such as OpenAI and Anthropic lies in their permanent positioning within the middle of the AI value-chain: selling APIs, model compute capacity and intermediate technical services without owning end-user-facing scenarios, direct-user relationships or native-distribution channels. In internet-era analogies, pure-model vendors correspond to modern-day web portals, traffic brokers and tool resellers. Despite measurable traffic volumes, invocation counts and high annualised run-rates, they remain transitional intermediaries dependent upon end-user platforms.

Internet-era ¡°shovel-selling¡± infrastructure providers (CDNs, server hosting, domain services) ultimately became low-margin commodity infrastructure whose profits were squeezed by platform operators. The fate awaiting AI-era pure-model vendors is more extreme still: whereas internet giants merely compressed channel margins, AI incumbents build capabilities in-house and eliminate intermediate markets entirely. Not even a low-margin ¡°shovel-selling¡± niche will remain for independent-model companies.

Ten years were required for the internet industry to complete intermediary-layer shake-out and solidify end-user-platform dominance. For AI, overlapping pressures of multi-year computing-power lock-ins, rigid instalment liabilities, high-cost competition and price-war margin destruction compress that decade-long industrial-convergence cycle down to 18-30-months. Internet-bubble collapse was driven by exhausted cash burn. The AI-bubble unwind will be triggered by four simultaneous forces: **maturity of computing-power contracts, closure of financing windows, completion of large-tech in-house-model closed-loops, and displacement by traditional-IT-sector alternatives.**

One core industrial law emerges: **AI¡¯s end-state equals an accelerated rerun of the internet-industry end-state. Organisations owning end-user products, user-facing scenarios, user bases and industrial-delivery capacity endure sustainably. Entities confined to intermediate-layer technology-and-compute sales face systematic elimination.**

## IV. Foundational Axiom: AI Possesses No Technical Moats ¡ª Only Capital Barriers; Technical Leadership Represents Merely a Short-Time Window Advantage
Market disorder, valuation bubbles, competitive intensity and corporate shake-out within AI all stem from one widely-overlooked fundamental truth: **The large-model industry contains no sustainable technical moats; it is fundamentally a capital-intensive game defined by capital burn.**

Traditional internet, software and search-engine industries feature genuine technical barriers: proprietary algorithms, long-accumulated datasets, user-network-effect advantages and iterative-legacy benefits that cannot be overcome purely through increased spending, creating natural catch-up gaps for late entrants. Large-model AI differs entirely. Its foundational building-blocks are open, replicable and transferable: the Transformer architecture, training paradigms and general-purpose datasets are publicly available; top-tier talent moves fluidly across organisations. Technical advantages held by leading-model providers contain no secret recipes, patent-locked innovations or irreplicable underlying architectures.

This yields one of AI¡¯s harshest iron laws: **Technical superiority enjoyed by pure-model enterprises lasts only 6-12-months; it is not a permanent moat.**

Industry-evolution evidence confirms this pattern. After GPT-4¡¯s launch, open-source models matched 80-% of its capability within three months and 90-% of general-purpose performance within six months. Microsoft closed the programming-performance gap set by Claude-3.5-Sonnet within half a year through MAI-Code and embedded it inside its native end-user ecosystem. Google¡¯s Gemini catch-up progress stemmed from in-house TPU-computing infrastructure and closed-loop proprietary data rather than exclusive technical breakthroughs.

What are commonly labelled ¡°entry-barriers¡± in AI boil down to three simple factors: abundant silicon chips, abundant electricity and abundant capital. These represent existential barriers for cash-constrained startups yet constitute ordinary operating expenses for large-technology groups. Microsoft, Google, ByteDance, Alibaba and Amazon execute multi-billion-dollar annual capital-expenditure programmes. Single large-model-training runs costing billions count as routine strategic investment with no material-funding pressure. Sums of this magnitude would consume multiple-years¡¯ worth of total-fundraising capacity for independent-model startups and lock-in crippling computing-power liabilities.

A fateful feedback loop traps pure--model enterprises: they hold only a narrow 6-12-month window of technical superiority in which to generate sufficient profits to cover multi-year future computing-power obligations. Yet large-tech in-house development, end-user-closed-loop construction and demand-diversion timelines precisely align with that window. Technical advantages expire, customer-substitution completes and core-revenue bases evaporate.

Deeper asset-asymmetry prevails. Core assets of independent-AI firms are replicable model weights and generic technical know-how with no exclusivity, proprietary rights or permanence. By contrast, large-tech¡¯s irreplicable moats consist of end-user populations, native scenarios, internally-generated datasets and in-house computing-power infrastructure. Traditional-IT-service providers¡¯ irreplaceable strengths lie in vertical-industry-delivery competence, compliance-and-operations expertise and accumulated government-enterprise trust. Neither set of durable advantages can be replicated by pure--model startups.

One critical distinction deserves emphasis: large-model AI is not a conventional asset-light industry. Conventional software and SaaS offerings are genuinely asset-light. Even eroding technical competitiveness leaves residual-customer-base stickiness and ongoing-maintenance revenue, granting extended runway for gradual decline. Large-model businesses operate within a heavy-capital framework burdened by multi-year non-cancellable instalment-based computing-power liabilities with zero operational buffer. Once elevated-growth market expectations collapse, private-market investment recedes and financing windows shut, no time remains for slow transformation or gradual contraction. Binding cash-flow constraints force rapid failure and market exit.

## V. Stratified Industrial End-State: Entities with Proprietary Foundations Persist; Downstream Pure-Sellers Face Time-Bound Shake-Out
Drawing upon four core pillars ¡ª computing-power liabilities, demand-structure analysis, historical-pattern recurrence and capital-system axioms ¡ª a polarised end-state for AI-industry participants is firmly established. Shake-out timelines are materially brought forward with no realistic reversal scenario.

1. **Category-1: Full-Stack Technology Giants (Permanent Players Focused on In-House-Use Scenarios)**
Microsoft, Google, ByteDance, Alibaba, Amazon and comparable groups own core native businesses including office productivity, search, cloud services, short-video platforms and e-commerce, plus massive genuine end-user-bases and internal-use scenarios. AI-models function merely as enabling tools for primary-business workflows, evolving primarily to satisfy internal workloads. Computing-power and model-development expenses are amortised across diversified primary-business scenarios. Surplus compute and model-capacity may be monetised through external sales. These firms face no existential operational-or-cash-flow pressures and hold long-term sustainable-growth potential plus absolute strategic initiative.

2. **Category-2: Traditional-IT-Industry Service Providers (Permanent Players Delivering Vertical-Industry Implementations)**
Established players such as Huawei, telecom carriers, Softstone, China National Software, Neusoft, Accenture and IBM possess decades-long vertical-sector experience across government-public-sector, manufacturing, finance and healthcare markets. They maintain mature project-delivery organisations, compliance capabilities, legacy-system-adaptation know-how and accumulated government-enterprise trust. They achieve industrial modernisation via embedded-AI upgrades and dominate implementation markets with ¡°vertical-industry-solution plus lightweight-AI¡± offerings. Stable low-margin cash-flow profiles grant them permanent industrial-relevance.

3. **Category-3: Downstream Pure-Model End-User-Oriented Enterprises (Transitional-Phase Participants)**
Pure--model firms including OpenAI and Anthropic own no native To-C products, no core To-B businesses and no internal workload-scenarios. 100-% of their revenue derives from third-party customer procurement. Burdened by five fatal handicaps ¡ª dual-layer asset bubbles, dual-sided-demand diversion by large-tech and traditional-IT competitors, collapse of high-end core-market segments, diseconomies of scale and absence of durable technical moats ¡ª they lack fundamentals supporting long-term viability. They represent transitional artifacts of AI-industry evolution and will undergo systematic market shake-out.
