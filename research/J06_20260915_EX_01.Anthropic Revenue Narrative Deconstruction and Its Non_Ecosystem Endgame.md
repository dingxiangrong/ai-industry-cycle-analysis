## Abstract

## I. Accounting Misrepresentation: Formally Compliant Yet Fundamentally Misleading Business Narrative

Under accounting standard ASC 606, Anthropic may qualify as a ¡°Seller of Record¡± and recognize gross revenue for transactions processed via cloud marketplaces, which is formally compliant. From a substantive industrial perspective, however, this constitutes deliberate narrative distortion: Anthropic treats platform-generated transaction volumes belonging to AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud as its own organic operating revenue, inflating its perceived business scale and growth outlook to mislead the market.

Critical commercial assets remain under cloud-provider control: enterprise client relationships, nationwide distribution networks, unified billing, compliance qualifications, and project delivery rights do not belong to model vendors. Funds circulate entirely within cloud-provider systems and never flow in full to Anthropic. Cloud platforms first deduct platform service fees, margins for ToB system integrators and channel distributors; only residual proceeds are remitted to the model vendor.

Public industry data corroborates this: over 40% of Anthropic¡¯s ARR comes from indirect cloud-channel sales, with annual channel-related payments to cloud providers reaching billions of US dollars. Its sensational reported revenue growth is essentially transactional dividends generated by cloud ecosystems, not value created by Anthropic¡¯s standalone operational capabilities.

## II. AWS¡¯s Core Moat Lies in a Closed-Loop Commercial Ecosystem, Not a Simple Online Storefront

A widespread industry misconception equates cloud-computing platforms with ordinary websites or web shops. The core competitive advantage of hyperscale clouds such as AWS is raw compute capacity; rather, it resides in exclusive, closed-loop, non-replicable ToB commercial ecosystem sovereignty.

First, top-tier platform operation capabilities: heavy ongoing investment in global infrastructure, brand credibility, enterprise compliance frameworks, and customer-onboarding workflows builds a high-confidence commercial marketplace that naturally aggregates large enterprise clients ¡ª capabilities entirely out of reach for pure-play model vendors.

Second, a mature multi-tier distribution system. Most enterprise custom projects, integration deployments, and government-enterprise deals are sourced and delivered by cloud-platform ecosystem partners, system integrators, and channel resellers. Distribution splits are determined through commercial negotiation and vary widely; 50/50 or 30/70 arrangements are common, and in extreme scenarios channel partners may capture up to 70% of a transaction¡¯s value.

Anthropic functions merely as a passive model producer hosted inside this ecosystem. It possesses no independent customer-acquisition capability, no channel leverage, and no pricing power. Within the value chain it receives only residual profits after successive layers of deductions.

## III. Structural Hard Risk: Hundreds-of-Billions-Dollar Compute Liabilities Constrain Underlying Profitability

This represents an irreversible structural predicament for model vendors. Anthropic is not a lightweight software firm; it is a long-term lessee of massive compute capacity. Under its decade-long commitment with AWS, Anthropic pledged over $100 billion in total compute spending, locking in up to 5 GW of Trainium-family chip capacity for training and inference workloads.

These costs are rigid, non-negotiable, and decoupled from business performance. Regardless of call volume, profitability, or industry cycles, committed compute payments must be honored. Even with zero end-users and zero orders, these multi-hundred-billion-dollar liabilities persist.

The modest residual payments received from cloud platforms must first cover these enormous compute obligations. Should demand fall short and compute capacity sit idle, revenue inversion and sustained structural losses follow, rendering a profitable business model fundamentally unviable.

## IV. Industrial Axioms: Zero LLM-API Stickiness and Non-Defensible Agents ¡ª Double-Layered Red-Ocean Competition

Setting aside softened vendor-centric narratives, three non-refutable J06 axioms frame core industrial realities and dismantle prevailing industry illusions:

**Axiom-J06-004 (Zero LLM-API Stickiness Axiom):** LLM APIs function as standardized substitutable commodities. Switching cost reduces to changing an endpoint and an API key; customer stickiness approaches zero. Customer data, conversation context, prompt engineering artifacts, tool-call assets, and client relationships reside within workflows and cloud platforms, never with the model vendor. LLM APIs carry virtually no brand-premium potential and operate within a brutal perfect-competition commodity market harsher than a conventional red ocean.

**Axiom-J06-005 (Non-Defensible-Agent Axiom):** Hopes that building Agents can offset API stickiness deficits constitute a major industry fallacy. Agent implementation barriers are substantially lower than foundation-model training; small teams can assemble functional Agents using open-source libraries and cloud-provider SDKs. More importantly, agent orchestration, memory management, context scheduling, and tool-chaining are becoming standardized platform features offered by AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud. Enterprises consume cloud-native Agent frameworks rather than vendor-locked agent products; Agents cannot form exclusive competitive moats.

**Axiom-J06-006 (Orchestration-Routing Monopoly Axiom):** End-customers belong to cloud providers; Agent frameworks belong to cloud providers; models act as replaceable commodities. Three compounded powers ¡ª traffic routing, model orchestration, billing settlement, and compliance governance ¡ª are monopolized by cloud vendors. Model vendors hold zero ecosystem sovereignty.

## V. Final Inference: The Growth Narrative for Ecosystem-Independent Model Vendors Has Collapsed

Markets commonly treat top-line reported revenue from ecosystem-hosted vendors such as Anthropic as evidence of permanently rising AI compute demand. This represents a fundamental, systemic misjudgment.

Total platform transaction value accrues to cloud ecosystems, not model suppliers. Remaining residual income after platform fees and channel distributions must service massive rigid compute liabilities. Meanwhile, near-zero API stickiness enables effortless customer churn, and the Agent path offers no durable differentiation. Both potential escape avenues for pure-play model vendors are effectively blocked.

Core conclusion: Customers belong to cloud platforms, not models. Cloud providers control absolute routing authority; both models and Agents serve as interchangeable underlying utilities.

Pure-play API-model vendors that operate without proprietary end-customer access, ecosystem sovereignty, or exclusive moats have reached the upper bound of their business-model potential; their long-term-growth expectations are unsustainable. Anthropic¡¯s postponement of next-generation foundation-model development, emphasis on safety-oriented messaging, and promotion of inflated top-line revenue figures reflect externalized operational risks, stalled growth, and a failing underlying business model.

The popular narrative of perpetually expanding AI compute demand drastically overstates the intrinsic industrial value of third-party model vendors. Long-term compute-growth projections require full re-examination and re-calibration.
